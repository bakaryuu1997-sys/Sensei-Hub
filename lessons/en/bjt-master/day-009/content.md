# 📚 BJT Master Business Japanese (N1 Elite Edition) - Day 9
## 🎯 Theme: Telephone Etiquette [Denwa Outai] - Taking Messages and the Art of Calling Back
### 📊 Target Level: J3 -> J2+ (Professional Foundation)

---

## 🪝 1. THE HOOK: The Nightmare of "I'll call you later"

Imagine you are working at a major corporation in Tokyo. The phone rings, and you pick up. On the other end is an important customer who wants to speak with your boss, but your boss is in a meeting.

You confidently say: **"Ato de denwa shimasu"**.

Congratulations, you've just recorded a "minus point" in the customer's eyes! For an N1 holder, the grammar above isn't wrong, but in **Business Japanese**, it is a subtle rudeness. Why? Because it lacks **"Kenjougo"** (humble language) and the initiative to confirm information. The customer will think: "When are they going to call back? Do they even have my number? Why use plain polite forms instead of humble ones?"

Today's lesson will help you break away from "textbook N1" and step into the world of professional telephone etiquette—where a single word can pivot an entire business relationship.

---

## 🎭 2. THE ANALOGY: The Mirror Reflecting the Company's Face

Telephone etiquette in a Japanese company is like an **"Acoustic Mirror."**

The caller cannot see your luxurious office or your polished suit. They only hear your voice, rhythm, and how you process information.
- If you answer hesitantly: The customer sees an unprofessional company.
- If you forget to write down the phone number: The customer sees a careless company.
- If you use honorifics incorrectly: The customer feels disrespected.

You are not just a person answering the phone; you are **"Kaisha no Kao"** (The face of the company). The telephone signal is the first "borderline" customers touch before deciding whether to sign a contract.

---

## ⚙️ 3. UNDER THE HOOD: The "Technique" of Reading the Air via Telephone

Why are Japanese people obsessed with answering the phone within **3 rings**?

It is the psychology of **"Matasenai"** (Not keeping them waiting). In the culture of **"Enryo"** (reserve/restraint), the caller often feels they are bothering the other person's time. Your quick pickup is an affirmation: "We are always ready to serve you."

When the intended recipient is absent, the technique of **"Omotenashi"** over the phone requires you to perform 4 "psychological" steps:
1. **Cushion Words**: To soften the refusal (the boss is absent). Example: **"Ainiku."**
2. **Clear Reason**: But not too detailed. Don't say "The boss is in the restroom"; say **"Seki wo hazushite orimasu"** (Away from desk).
3. **Repetition (Fukushou)**: To affirm "I am listening to you seriously."
4. **Proposal to Call Back**: To show initiative to the customer.

---

## 🎭 4. SIMULATED DIALOGUE: Taking a Message

**Situation:** A customer from Tanaka Shoji calls to speak with Manager Yamada (who is in a meeting). You (An) are the one receiving the call.

**(Phone rings twice)**

**An:** Thank you for calling. This is Supernova Co., Ltd.
**Customer:** Thank you for your continued support. This is Sato from Tanaka Shoji.
**An:** Mr. Sato, thank you for your continued support as well.
**Customer:** Is Manager Yamada available?
**An:** **Unfortunately**, Yamada is currently **in a meeting** and is **away from his desk**. He is expected to be back around 15:00.
**Customer:** I see. Well, when he returns, I would like him to call me.
**An:** Certainly. Just to be sure, may I have your phone number, Mr. Sato?
**Customer:** It's 03-1234-5678.
**An:** Let me repeat that. 03-1234-5678. I will **relay the message** to Yamada to **call you back** as soon as he returns. This is An speaking.

---

### 🔍 Nuance Analysis

1.  **"Ainiku"**: This is the ultimate **Cushion Word**. It shows regret for not meeting the customer's request without needing a long explanation.
2.  **"Yamada wa"**: When speaking to an outsider about someone within your own organization, never use titles (Manager Yamada) or -san. This is the **"Uchi-Soto"** rule.
3.  **"Seki wo hazushite orimasu"**: The safest phrase when someone is not at their desk.
4.  **"O-denwa wo sashiageru"**: The humble form of "making a phone call."
5.  **"Uketamawarimashita"**: Shows a formal acceptance. It carries more weight than "Wakarimashita."

## 🧠 5. EXAM LOGIC: The "First Meeting" Trap on the BJT

In the BJT exam, the Listening section often presents phone situations where the caller is calling for the **FIRST TIME**.

**Trap 1**: Forgetting to use **"O-namae wo ukagatte mo yoroshii deshō ka"** (May I ask your name?). If the customer hasn't introduced themselves, you cannot say "Who's this?". You must use the most polite humble/interrogative form.

**Trap 2**: Confusing **"Tsutaeru"** and **"Moushi-tsutaeru"**. When promising the customer to relay a message to your boss, you must use **"Moushi-tsutaemasu"**.

**Trap 3**: Processing order. The BJT often asks: "What is the first thing to do after knowing the boss is absent?". The answer is usually: **Ask if the customer wants to leave a message or receive a callback**, instead of just hanging up or making them wait long.

## 📝 6. QUICK QUIZ

**Q1**: You receive a call for a colleague named Suzuki. Suzuki is out meeting a partner. Which way of saying it is most appropriate?
A) Suzuki-san is currently out.
B) Suzuki is currently out. (**Humble form**)
C) Manager Suzuki is unfortunately out.
D) Suzuki-san is away from his desk.

**Q2**: When you cannot hear the customer's name clearly over the phone, what should you say to remain polite?
A) What did you say?
B) Sorry, once more please.
C) The phone seems a bit distant, but may I ask your name once more?
D) I can't hear your name.

**Q3**: The customer requests: "Please give me a callback." How should you confirm?
A) Understood. I'll make him call.
B) Certainly. I will relay the message to Yamada to give you a callback.
C) Understood. Mr. Yamada will call.

> **Answers: Q1:B, Q2:C, Q3:B**
> **Deep Explanation**: The phrase **"The phone seems a bit distant"** is the pinnacle of **Euphemism**, politely blaming the technology instead of the customer's voice.
