# 📚 BJT Master Business Japanese (N1 Elite Edition) - Day 28
## 🎯 Theme: Internal Conflict and Mediation - Choosing Words to Resolve Disagreement and Maintain Organizational Harmony
### 📊 Target Level: J2 -> J1+ (Mediator & Harmony Specialist Mastery)

---

## 🪝 1. THE HOOK: When the "Mediator" Becomes the "Culprit"

During a meeting, two of your colleagues, Sato and Kim, are arguing heatedly over the implementation plan of a project. Sato (the senior) insists on using the old method, while Kim (the junior) wants to innovate. You step in to stop them:
**"Come on now, both of you calm down. Let's stop fighting. The Manager will be troubled by this."**

The result: Sato glares at you: "Do you think we're children having a playground fight?" Kim falls silent but looks deeply frustrated. The tension, far from decreasing, has spread to include you.

Why did your mediation fail? It's because you used the language of **"Emotion"** (fighting/Kenka) instead of the language of **"Business."** In the world of J1+, a clash is not a "fight," it is a **"Difference of Opinion" (Iken no Soui)**.

A J1+ professional doesn't tell others to "calm down." They perform **"Traffic Coordination" (Koutsuu Seiri)** of the different flows of ideas, find common ground, and provide an honorable exit for both parties.

---

## 🎭 2. THE ANALOGY: The Welder and the Sparks

Internal conflict is like two pieces of metal being **welded together**.
- Sparks (arguments) are inevitable when there is a strong collision between ideas.
- **The N1-Level Employee**: Stands by in fear, trying to throw water on the fire (suppressing emotions). The result is that the weld cools down and the pieces don't stick (the problem isn't solved).
- **The J1+ Level Master**: Is a **"Master Welder."**
    - They are not afraid of sparks.
    - They use a protective mask (composure) to look through the sparks and see the true "Joint."
    - They adjust the temperature (choice of words) so that the opinions melt together, forming a structure stronger than the original parts.

The goal of mediation is not to silence people, but to reach a **"Consensus" (Goui Keisei)**.

---

## ⚙️ 3. UNDER THE HOOD: Strategies of the Mid-level Mediator

To resolve conflict professionally, you must master the arts of **"Neutrality" (Chuuritsu)** and **"Justification" (Taigi Meibun)**.

1. **The Art of Reframing**: Instead of saying "Person A says Person B is wrong," say: **"It seems that the starting points (standpoints) of you two are different."** This slides the conversation from a personal attack to a discussion of methodology.
2. **Returning to Common Interest**: Always remind everyone of the ultimate goal (**Kyoutsuu no Gooru**). For example: "Whichever method we choose, we both want the customer to be most satisfied, right?"
3. **Protecting Face (Mentsu)**: Never judge who is right or wrong in front of the crowd. J1+ professionals use phrases like: **"Both plans have their merits (Ichiri arimasu)."**
4. **Proposing a "Landing Point"**: Find a Plan C that combines the advantages of A and B.

## 🎭 4. SIMULATED DIALOGUE: Mediating a Conflict of Opinions

**Situation**: Sato and Kim are debating whether to lower the price to keep a client. Sato wants to lower it, Kim wants to keep the price to protect the brand.

**Sato**: Just this once, we should offer a 5% discount to prevent them from going to a competitor!

**Kim**: But if we lower the price once, a drop in brand image is inevitable. I am against an easy price cut!

**An**: **Your enthusiastic debate is very helpful for reference**. Both of your opinions come from thinking about the company's profit first.

**Sato**: What do you think, An?

**An**: I believe Sato's **sense of crisis** about "securing the client now" and Kim's **perspective** of "protecting long-term value" are both indispensable elements.

**Kim**: But, An...

**An**: **To summarize the points**, I believe the argument boils down to "short-term sales" versus "long-term brand." Therefore, **as a compromise (Secchuu-an)**, how about we offer additional services for a limited time instead of lowering the price? This way, we can respond to the client's request without lowering the profit margin.

**Sato**: Hmm, additional services... that might be worth considering.

**Kim**: In that case, the brand image can also be protected, so I can agree to that.

**An**: Thank you. **I feel that by combining the wisdom of you two, a better solution has come into view**. Let's consult the manager with this plan.

---

### 🔍 Nuance Analysis

1.  **"Very helpful for reference"**: A phrase that defuses tension. It upgrades a "clash" into a "constructive discussion."
2.  **"Sense of crisis" vs. "Perspective"**: Assigning positive labels to each side's opinion (one side is risk-sensitive, the other has a long-term vision) allows both to feel their personal dignity is respected.
3.  **"To summarize the points" (Seiri sasete itadakimasu)**: The move of a potential leader. You strip away emotional words and leave only the core of the problem.
4.  **"Compromise" (Secchuu-an)**: A key J1+ vocabulary word. It shows you've thought of a third solution that harmonizes both sides.
5.  **"The result of combining the wisdom of you two"**: An incredibly smart closing. You don't say "I thought of it," you say "This is the result of your combined wisdom." This re-bonds the relationship that was strained.

## 🧠 5. EXAM LOGIC: The Logic of "Mediation" in BJT Section 3

In BJT, conflict situations are often placed in emails or long dialogues to test **Information Filtering**.

- **Trap 1: Choosing a Side**: The exam asks: "What should An do?" If the answer is "Support Sato" or "Support Kim," it is INCORRECT. The correct answer always involves **"Coordination" (Chousei)** or **"Re-examination" (Saikentou)**.
- **Trap 2: Keigo Errors During Temper**: The exam might present a staff member using incorrect keigo while arguing (e.g., using honorifics for themselves). You must identify this and choose the correct correction.
- **Trap 3: Ultimate Goal**: BJT tests whether you understand the **ultimate interest of the company**. Any mediation that harms the company (even if the two people make up) is not the best result.

## 📝 6. QUICK QUIZ

**Q1**: Two colleagues are disagreeing over a minor detail. What should you say to make them stop?
A) Such minor things don't matter.
B) **"Shall we return to the overall goal for a moment? Let's align the minor methods later."**
C) I'll decide which one is right.
D) You'll be scolded by the Manager.

**Q2**: A colleague is shouting at you in the workplace due to a misunderstanding. What is the most "Elite" way to react?
A) Shout back so you don't get bullied.
B) Walk away.
C) **"First, listen to the other party's side until the end, accept it by saying 'I understand what you're saying,' and then calmly perform a fact check."**
D) Cry to get people's sympathy.

**Q3**: Which word in business refers to "aligning different opinions into one unified opinion"?
A) Kenka (Fighting)
B) Oshaberi (Chatting)
C) **Suriawase (Aligning/Coordination)**
D) Meirei (Command)

---

### 💡 Deep Explanation

**Q1: B**
- Uses the phrase **"Returning to the overall goal" (Zentai no mokuteki ni tachikaeru)**. This is how to pull people out of the emotional trap and back into business thinking. Minor points are handled with the professional term **"Suriawase."**

**Q2: C**
- This is the technique of **"Assertive Communication."** Using the phrase **"Accepting" (Uketomeru)** lowers the other party's anger immediately. Then, basing the solution on **"Fact checking"** resolves the misunderstanding fundamentally.

**Q3: C**
- **"Suriawase"** is the soul of Japanese management. it's not about one side winning and the other losing, but grinding down the sharp edges of two opinions so they fit together perfectly. This is a must-know word for J1+.
